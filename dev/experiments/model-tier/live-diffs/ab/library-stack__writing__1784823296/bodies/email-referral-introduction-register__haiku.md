## Overview

A warm introduction or referral email involves three parties: the introducer, the person being introduced, and the new contact. When you receive such an introduction, your reply must serve dual purpose — you accept the new contact's offer for connection while also acknowledging and thanking the person who cc'd you both. A single short message handles both tasks simultaneously because the introducer remains on the thread and watches your reply.

## Key Concepts

This register differs from [[email-professional-courtesy-register|professional courtesy email]] in one critical way: you have a cc'd party who has already done you a favor by making the connection. Your response is not a standalone exchange with one reader; it is observed by a benefactor. This creates competing demands — you must be warm and efficient for the new contact while also crediting the introducer's role [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email].

The dual-audience problem shapes every line. The introducer's hand-off statement typically names the connection (who the new contact is), states the fit (why you two should talk), and asks the new contact to reach out directly, cc'ing both of you in the initial approach [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email]. Your reply confirms that approach worked.

## Patterns

**Open with gratitude and warmth.** Lead with thanks and a brief warmth line—"Great to be connected"—before shifting to logistics [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email]. This move tells the introducer you recognize their effort, not as an aside in the closing, but as the primary reason for the message.

**Accept with a concrete next step tied to the mechanism offered.** If the new contact offered a scheduling link, your reply should confirm you will use it: "I will find a spot on your calendar" rather than a vague "I'll be in touch." The specificity ties your acceptance to their actual offer, making the handoff clear [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email].

**Provide a concrete scheduling mechanism, not open-ended availability.** If you are the one offering the next step, hand over a scheduling link rather than trading open-ended availability back and forth. This accelerates agreement and removes barriers to booking [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email].

**Keep the sign-off short and first-person.** A greeting like "Hi [Name]-" holds the warm register in this context, consistent with [[email-professional-courtesy-register|professional courtesy email conventions]] [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email].

## Form Conventions

The referral-acceptance email compresses what would normally take multiple exchanges into one reply. Because the introducer is watching, you cannot defer thanks or hide the scheduling logistics in a follow-up. Unlike [[email-internal-work-request-register|internal work requests]], which front status before the ask, a referral acceptance leads with gratitude and embeds the scheduling agreement immediately after. The entire message should fit comfortably in 4–5 short sentences.

## Examples

**Good:**

> Hi Sarah,
>
> Great to be connected — thank you for the introduction. I will find a spot on your calendar for next week. Looking forward to talking.
>
> Best,
> Alex

This reply opens with thanks, names the introducer by acknowledging her role, accepts the scheduling handoff with a concrete commitment tied to Sarah's offered mechanism (the calendar link), and closes warmly.

**Poor:**

> Thank you. I will fill out a spot.
>
> Alex

This reply fails on two counts. First, "fill out a spot" is a broken idiom — you fill out a *form*, but you find or grab a *spot* on a calendar. The confusion muddles the logistics. Second, and more damaging, the message thanks no one. The introducer who arranged the connection is cc'd and watching; this reply omits any acknowledgment of her effort, leaving her uncertain whether the introduction landed [exemplar-referral-acknowledgment-email].

## Pitfalls

**Vague closings.** "I will be in touch" or "Let's connect soon" leave the new contact waiting for you to move next, prolonging the exchange and adding friction. A concrete commitment tied to the mechanism the other party offered closes the loop immediately.

**Omitting the introducer by name.** Even a glancing "Thank you for the introduction" is better than silence; it tells the cc'd party the connection registered. Naming the introducer directly is warmer and clearer.

**Hidden scheduling logistics.** Hiding the next step in a follow-up email, or mentioning it only in passing, creates ambiguity. State the concrete mechanism — the calendar link, the meeting room, the time window — in the main sentence.

## Field Notes

This email's efficiency comes from the three-party constraint: the introducer is present and has already moved the ball, so you cannot afford to be tentative or leave loose ends. The warmth and concreteness work together — openness to gratitude makes the logistics feel less transactional, and clear logistics make the gratitude feel genuine rather than obligatory.
